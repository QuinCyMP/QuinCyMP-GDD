# Selling or handing over the design

Read when writing anything under `pitch/`, or when the user asks about selling the idea, finding a publisher, or rights.

## The first truth, said plainly

**The market for a bare design document barely exists.** Nearly every studio refuses outside ideas as policy, to avoid "I thought of it first" lawsuits, and because ideas are not what they lack. They lack production capacity and confidence that it will work.

That is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to pick a path with a real market. There are five:

| Path | What is really sold | Odds | Needed beyond the GDD |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GDD plus a playable prototype** | Proof it is fun | Highest | A vertical slice playable for 10 to 20 minutes |
| **IP plus an art bible** | World, characters, atmosphere | Medium | Distinctive art and a complete story |
| **Joining a team as a designer** | You, with the document as portfolio | High | A document that shows how you think, not just results |
| **Selling one system or spec** | A single system designed in depth | Medium | A spec plus data tables that can be implemented at once |
| **Finding co-founders to build it** | A vision and a believable scope | High | A roadmap that proves you understand the constraints |

What raises a document's value most, by real effect:

1. A playable prototype, even grey boxes. It turns "an idea" into "a thing".
2. Evidence people want it: wishlists, Discord members, clips people watched.
3. Complete data tables. They show the thinking is finished, not just the shell.
4. An honest risk section. Buyers look for authors who know.
5. A scope small enough to believe.

## Three buyers, three briefs

Write one `pitch/buyer-brief.md` per recipient. Never send the same version to everyone.

### A. Publisher or investor

The question in their head: "How much comes back, when, and what is the risk?"

Must have: the target market in numbers (what comparable games sold, at what price, with how many reviews); budget and time to release; revenue model with a conservative forecast; the team and who can really deliver (the weakest point of a solo developer, so it needs a plan); risks and mitigations.

Leave out long system descriptions. They do not read them.

### B. Studio or IP buyer

The question: "Can we build this as is, or does it need redesigning?"

Must have: systems specified well enough that a programmer starts without asking; the tables in `data/`; the technical demands of the design; and clarity about **who owns this work**. If an artist was ever hired or a friend ever co-designed, clear it first. Unclear rights sink more deals than quality does.

### C. Co-founder or teammate

The question: "Will I waste two years on this person?"

Must have: a vision clear enough that they see the same picture; a scope small enough to believe it finishes; roles (who does what, who decides); and an agreement on shares and rights from day one, before any work starts.

## Send in order, never the whole document first

1. **A short email with the logline** (5 lines). See whether they reply.
2. **The one-pager**, if they are interested.
3. **A conversation.** Let them ask.
4. **The full document**, once there is a relationship and the basic terms have been discussed.

A 40-page document attached to the first email is never opened, and you lose the chance to learn why they said no.

## Rights: common practice, not legal advice

These are industry practices. A real contract or a deal with money gets reviewed by an IP lawyer, because details differ by country.

- **Copyright protects expression, not ideas.** Nobody can own "an MMO where things decay". The document, text, images, proper names and data tables you wrote are yours the moment they are written.
- **Create proof of date.** Keep the work in git with its commit history, or email it to yourself. Free, and useful.
- **NDAs have limited value.** Large studios usually will not sign before seeing the work; those who sign are usually small. Do not let a missing NDA stop a conversation, and do not send the most important material before there is a relationship.
- **Watch what is not yours.** Reference images from the web, AI-generated work, names that clash with trademarks. Clear them before the document reaches a buyer. Credit the source of every image.
- **Deals that actually happen.** Outright sales are rare. Common: hired as the designer, revenue share, credit plus milestone payments. Set expectations there.

## Pitch deck outline, 10 to 12 slides

| # | Slide | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title and logline | One sentence, one image |
| 2 | The problem, the gap | What frustrates the target player in existing games |
| 3 | The One Thing | The single difference, with an image or clip |
| 4 | Core loop | What the player repeats |
| 5 | World and atmosphere | Art, not text |
| 6 | Three key systems | Two lines each |
| 7 | Market and comparable games | Sales and player numbers of similar games |
| 8 | Revenue model | |
| 9 | Scope and timeline | Vertical slice to first release |
| 10 | Team and current status | What already exists |
| 11 | The ask | How much money, which people, what exactly. Be precise. |

Slide 11 is the one people forget most, and the reason meetings end with nothing happening.

## Checklist before anything leaves the building

- [ ] The one-pager reads in 2 minutes and explains the game.
- [ ] Real market numbers from comparable games, not "the MMO market is worth billions".
- [ ] The ask is explicit.
- [ ] Every image has a known source and the right to use it.
- [ ] There is proof of date.
- [ ] The version sent matches the recipient type (A, B or C).
- [ ] The risk section is still there, not deleted to look better.
